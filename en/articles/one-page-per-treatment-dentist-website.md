## Patients search for a treatment, not a practice

"Implant dentaire" (dental implant) is searched 33,100 times a month in France (Semrush, August 2026). Almost always, the patient adds their city, a question or the word "cost". They don't type "dental practice". They have a missing tooth, a treatment estimate to compare, and they are looking for the treatment that solves their problem.

Google and AI tools follow the patient. For a search about a treatment, they answer with the page that covers exactly that treatment. Your home page talks about everything: the team, the hours, ten treatments in one line each. It ranks for your name, rarely for a treatment. Your "dental implant" page, on the other hand, can answer "dental implant" plus your city, "what happens during implant placement" and "dental implant pain". One page, one treatment, dozens of patient questions. For a practice that places a lot of implants, the article on [SEO for an implant dentist](https://en.developpia.fr/blog/dental-implant-seo-one-hundred-implants-a-year/) goes further.

Our [dental practice website](https://en.developpia.fr/dental-practice-website/) page explains why a website is built with one page per treatment. This article opens one of those pages and looks at what it must contain.

## What a treatment page must contain, in order

A good treatment page follows the thread of the patient's questions. First they want to know whether the treatment is right for them, then what will happen, then what it costs. Here are the nine sections, in this order:

1. **Who the treatment is for.** One missing tooth, several teeth, a removable denture that moves. The patient should recognize themselves in the first few lines.
2. **The process, step by step.** Consultation, exam and X-rays, treatment estimate, placement, healing, crown, follow-up. Each step in two or three sentences.
3. **Duration and number of visits.** How many appointments, how long between each one, the total duration, using your own timelines.
4. **Recovery and pain.** What the patient feels afterward, for how long, what they can eat, when they go back to work. This is often the missing section.
5. **Alternatives.** The bridge (a fixed prosthesis anchored on the neighboring teeth), the removable denture, doing nothing. Saying that other solutions exist is part of fair information.
6. **When the treatment is not possible.** Insufficient bone without a graft, certain medical conditions, heavy smoking, growth not yet complete. The patient then calls with the right questions.
7. **Fees, or a range with a treatment estimate.** The amount or the range for a simple case, what it covers, and the treatment estimate given after the exam. Article R.4127-240 of the French Public Health Code requires you to inform patients about your fees.
8. **Patients' frequently asked questions.** Three to six questions, the ones you get in the chair, with short answers.
9. **Booking an appointment.** Phone, address, online booking, and the reason for the visit: "first implant consultation".

## Informing without persuading: the rules of the game

A treatment page is not a sales page. Since [decree no. 2020-1658 of December 22, 2020](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000042730959), dentists may inform the public about their practice, including on a website. What remains forbidden is specific: obtaining a priority spot in the results for payment (R.4127-217 II), and advertising for a third party or a commercial company (R.4127-225). Article R.4127-215-1 sets the frame: fair and honest communication, with no third-party testimonials, no comparison with other dentists, no encouragement of unnecessary treatment.

In practice, on a treatment page:

- no patient testimonials, not even anonymous ones;
- no before-and-after photos: the code does not name them, but the French Dental Council (Ordre national des chirurgiens-dentistes) cites them as an example of what can suggest a guaranteed result, and Luxembourg bans them;
- no sentence that pushes toward treatment: "get your smile back today" is advertising copy, "an implant replaces the root of a missing tooth" is information;
- no title you do not hold: the code reserves titles for three disciplines, dentofacial orthopedics (orthodontics), oral surgery and oral medicine. A general dentist who places implants writes "implant placement".

These rules do not make the page poorer. They make it credible. A patient who reads about recovery, limits and alternatives can tell they are being told the truth. The guide [what the Dental Council allows on a dentist website](https://en.developpia.fr/guides/what-the-dental-council-allows-on-a-dentist-website/) goes through each rule with examples.

> **The key takeaway**
> Patients search for a treatment and a city, not a practice. The page that describes that treatment, in the order of their questions and within the Dental Council rules, is the one Google ranks, AI tools cite and patients read before calling.

## A sample outline for a "dental implant" page, for a fictional practice

Take a fictional practice, the Tilleuls practice in Angers. It places implants and wants a page for patients in the city. The page title: "Dental implant in Angers: process, recovery and fees". The first paragraph says in three sentences what an implant is, who it is for and where the practice is. Then the headings, in order:

- "When an implant is recommended";
- "How placement works, step by step";
- "How many visits and how long it takes", with the timelines the practice actually observes, without promises;
- "After placement: what you will feel";
- "Other solutions";
- "When an implant is not possible";
- "Fees", with the range for a single implant, crown included, what it covers, the treatment estimate and the date it was last updated;
- "Your questions", five patient questions;
- "Book an appointment", with the visit reason "first implant consultation".

Each heading uses the patient's words, not yours. Write "how long it takes" rather than "treatment timeline", "after placement" rather than "postoperative course". Technical terms are still useful, but explained in parentheses.

## The treatment pages your practice should have

Eight to ten treatment pages are enough for a practice. The choice depends on what you do.

For a general practice:

- cleaning (scaling) and prevention;
- cavity treatment and composite fillings;
- root canal treatment;
- crowns and bridges;
- extractions and wisdom teeth;
- gum treatment (periodontics);
- whitening;
- removable dentures;
- dental emergencies;
- children's dentistry.

For an implant practice:

- single implant;
- multiple implants and implant-supported bridge;
- full-arch prosthesis on implants;
- bone graft and sinus lift;
- extraction and immediate implant;
- immediate loading (teeth placed the day of surgery);
- implant crown;
- implant maintenance and follow-up;
- veneers;
- fees and insurance coverage.

One page per line, each with its own address, its own title and the name of the city. If two treatments are too similar to make two honest pages, keep just one.

## The same page, the Google Business Profile and AI search

Your treatment page does not live on its own. On your Google Business Profile, you can list your services. Use the same words as your pages: "dental implant" on the profile, in the page title and in the booking reason. Google ranks the map using three official criteria, [relevance, distance and prominence](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en) (Google help). Relevance is the match between what the patient types and what your profile and website say. A profile that says "implantology" and a website that talks about "dental implant" are not saying the same thing to a machine. Our [Google Business Profile for dentists](https://en.developpia.fr/google-business-profile-dentist/) page details this link.

AI tools point the same way. 56.6% of French people already use artificial intelligence, including to find a dentist (Arcom and Médiamétrie, April 2026). Since [July 22, 2026](https://blog.google/intl/fr-fr/nouveautes-produits/explorez-obtenez-des-reponses/recherche-ia-apercus-mode/), Google has been showing its AI Overviews and AI Mode in France. When a patient asks "where to get an implant in Angers", the AI looks for a page that clearly states three things: the treatment, the city, the practice. One clear page, one treatment, one city: that is easier to cite than a home page that mixes everything. There is also a technical condition. The text must be in the page itself, not displayed by a script (a small program that fills in the page after it loads), because the bots from OpenAI, Anthropic and Perplexity do not read JavaScript. Our [AI search for dentists](https://en.developpia.fr/ai-search-dentist/) page explains how these bots read a website.

## The mistakes that empty a treatment page

Some treatment pages exist and serve no purpose. Here are the ones we see most often. For the website as a whole, see also [the seven mistakes on dental practice websites](https://en.developpia.fr/blog/seven-mistakes-on-dental-practice-websites/).

- The single "Our treatments" page, with ten treatments in ten lines. It answers no specific search.
- Text copied from an implant manufacturer or another website. Google recognizes the duplicate, and the patient doesn't find your way of working.
- The page with no city. A patient looks for a treatment near them. Without the city name, the page competes with all of France.
- The page with no fees or treatment estimate. The patient leaves to find the price elsewhere, often at another practice.
- The page full of jargon: "implant-supported rehabilitation" with no translation. The patient doesn't recognize themselves, and neither does the AI.

Each one can be fixed in a single work session. Start with the treatment your patients ask about most, often implants, and write its page following the nine sections. The others follow the same outline. This is the foundation of [dental SEO](https://en.developpia.fr/dental-seo/): pages that each answer one specific question. The next one to write is the one your patients ask about right after implants.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I have one page per treatment or a single "our treatments" page?

One page per treatment. Patients type a specific treatment, and both Google and AI tools answer with the page that covers it. A single page listing everything ranks for nothing. Keep the "our treatments" page as a table of contents, with a link to each treatment page.

### How long should a treatment page be?

As long as it takes to answer the patient's nine questions: who the treatment is for, the process, the duration, recovery, alternatives, limits, fees, frequently asked questions and booking. In practice, that is about the length of a blog article. A shorter page leaves questions unanswered; a longer one reads poorly on a phone.

### Can I describe my implant work without advertising?

Yes. The decree of December 22, 2020 allows information about your practice, and describing a treatment is part of that. The limit is the tone: you describe what you do, recovery, limits and fees, with no testimonials, no before-and-after photos and no sentence that pushes toward treatment. If in doubt, your local (departmental) Dental Council can review the page.
